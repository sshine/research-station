This is a practical synthesis of the manufacturer’s instructions, material data, the professional carpet-cleaning standard, and Danish rental/service prices — not a laboratory test of this particular rug.

Bottom line

  1. “Kan ikke vaskes” / “Do not wash” means do not machine-wash or fully immerse the rug. IKEA’s own care FAQ renders the instruction as “Do not machine wash”, and the same product page tells you to clean spots with a damp cloth and a mild detergent. Water is not forbidden; excess water and slow drying are. IKEA DK [1] · IKEA care FAQ [2]
  2. “Professionel tæpperens” is water-based carpet cleaning, not dry cleaning. IKEA separately says “Tåler ikke kemisk rens” / “Do not dry clean.” For this rubber-backed construction, the sensible professional reference is the carpet-cleaning trade (typically hot-water extraction or a low-moisture method), not a dry-cleaning shop and not a rug-washing plant that fully immerses the rug. That is an inference: IKEA does not define “professional,” but it does say “do not wash” and separately allows professional carpet cleaning. IKEA DK [1]
  3. You can reproduce most of the professional process at home with a wet/dry shop vacuum, a clean sprayer, a soft brush, white towels, and a fan. A rented carpet extractor is closer to professional hot-water extraction. The “professional” part is the sequence and the discipline — pre-vacuum, controlled chemistry, dwell, gentle agitation, thorough extraction, rinsing, and fast drying — not a secret chemical.
  4. For this 130 cm rug (1.33 m²), hiring a professional may cost about the same as renting a machine. Professional cleaning is roughly 250–725 DKK depending on the provider; a 4-hour/day rental plus cleaning fluid is roughly 316–415 DKK, plus travel, deposit, and your time. If you already own a wet/dry vacuum, DIY is much cheaper. Tæpperenseriet [3] · Rene Tæpper [4] · MegaRens [5] · Silvan [6]
  5. The main risks are over-wetting the rubber backing, heat, oil-based solvents, detergent residue, and slow drying. If the backing is cracked, peeling, sticky, or the rug has a persistent odour, do not wet-clean it yourself; spot-clean only or get it assessed.

1. What IKEA’s instructions actually mean

The STOENSE 704.268.01 label says:

Material: Slitageside: 100 % polypropylen. Bagsidebelægning: 100 % Syntetisk gummi.

Vedligeholdelse: Kan ikke vaskes. Tåler ikke klorblegning. Må ikke tørres i tørretumbler. Må ikke stryges. Tåler ikke kemisk rens. Støvsug tæppet og vend det ofte. Tørre pletter fjernes straks ved at skrabe forsigtigt mod midten af pletten. Gnid ikke på våde pletter. Sug væsken op med køkkenrulle, fugt med en klud og et mildt rengøringsmiddel. Professionel tæpperens efter behov. Brug altid det almindelige støvsugerhoved til gulve, når du støvsuger tæppet, aldrig den roterende børste.

IKEA DK product page [1]

The English version uses the same structure: “Do not wash”, “Do not dry clean”, “Professional carpet cleaning when needed.” IKEA US [7]

Three separate instructions are being conflated in the question:

  • “Kan ikke vaskes” / “Do not wash” — no machine washing or full immersion. IKEA’s own care FAQ says “Do not machine wash”, not “never get it wet.” (The FAQ is a market-specific IKEA page, but it is the clearest official gloss on the wording.) IKEA care FAQ [2]
  • “Tåler ikke kemisk rens” / “Do not dry clean” — no solvent dry cleaning. This is a separate prohibition, and it is why “professional” cannot mean “take it to the dry cleaner.”
  • “Professionel tæpperens efter behov” — an allowed escalation to the carpet-cleaning trade when vacuuming and spot cleaning are not enough.

So there is no contradiction. The label forbids the two methods that would soak or solvent-treat the whole rug, while explicitly allowing controlled damp cleaning of spots and professional water-based carpet cleaning when needed. IKEA’s general rug guide (written around Persian rugs) makes the same distinction: wash only when vacuuming cannot remove the dirt, and use a professional when special equipment is needed. IKEA Canada rug guide [8]

The missing piece is that IKEA does not define which professional method or which synthetic rubber. The rest of this report fills that gap conservatively.

2. Why the backing, not the polypropylene, is the limiting part

The face fibre is water-friendly but oil-loving and heat-sensitive

Polypropylene (also called olefin) is hydrophobic: it absorbs very little water, so most water-based spills sit on the surface and blot up easily. It is also solution-dyed, so the colour is unusually stable. Ochoco [9] · Masterful Carpet Cleaning [10]

Its weaknesses are equally important:

  • It is oleophilic. It attracts and holds oils, so greasy soils are the hard case. Cleaning Systems: “Polypropylene is oleophilic. That means that it has a strong natural affinity for oils. It attracts oily soils.” Cleaning Systems [11] Bane-Clene makes the same point. Bane-Clene [12]
  • It softens with heat. INEOS gives a melting point of 160–165 °C and a Vicat softening temperature of 152 °C for polypropylene; fibre-grade PP has a softening point around 150 °C. INEOS datasheet [13] · Swicofil [14] Those temperatures are far above normal cleaning, but they explain why an iron, a hot hairdryer held close, a steam mop, or friction heat from a rotary brush can permanently distort the pile. Sutherlandshire: high-temperature steam “can partially melt and distort polypropylene pile, causing permanent matting or texture change.” Sutherlandshire [15]
  • It has poor crush recovery. Heavy furniture and traffic can flatten it, and some flattening is permanent. Cleaning Systems [11]
  • Oil-based solvents can make things worse. They may lift the stain but leave a new oil residue that is equally hard to remove. Sutherlandshire [15]

The synthetic rubber backing is the real reason for “do not wash”

IKEA says only “100 % Syntetisk gummi”; it does not name the polymer. The common carpet backing materials are styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) latex, carboxylated SBR, or thermoplastic rubber blends. The general behaviour of latex/SBR-backed carpet is well documented:

  • Latex adhesive loses strength when wet and regains most of its strength when dry. C&R: “a carpet’s latex adhesive loses its strength when wet, but its strength returns once dried.” C&R Magazine [16]
  • Fillers in the adhesive “do not respond well to excessive water and tend to soften when saturated.” The same article lists “overly aggressive extraction”, “excessive folding or stretching”, and “excessive heat” as causes of delamination. C&R Magazine [16]
  • NC State Extension: “Moisture can weaken the latex backing and walking on the carpet can cause the backing to separate.” NC State Extension [17]
  • A rubber-backed rug is non-breathable and can trap moisture between the rug and the floor, causing dampness and discoloration. RugPadUSA [18]

This is why the face fibre’s water tolerance is not the whole answer. Polypropylene can tolerate water; the backed construction may not tolerate prolonged water, heat, or aggressive extraction. The manufacturer’s “do not wash” is a construction-specific instruction, and it outranks the generic “polypropylene can be hosed down” advice.

The disagreement you will find online

Many polypropylene guides say the rug can be fully saturated or hosed down. For example, Rug Branch says “Polypropylene handles getting fully saturated without any fibre damage” and gives a hose-down method. Rug Branch [19] That statement is about the yarn. It does not account for a rubber backing. For this rug, do not hose it down, do not soak it, and do not machine-wash it. A professional cleaner who says “polypropylene is waterproof” may be right about the fibre and wrong about the rug.

3. What “professional carpet cleaning” actually is

Professional carpet cleaning is a process standard, not a brand or a single machine. The relevant standard is ANSI/IICRC S100, Standard for Professional Cleaning of Textile Floor Coverings. It defines the procedures, methods, chemistry, fibre/construction evaluation, equipment, safety, inspections, spot removal, and area-rug cleaning. IICRC S100 [20]

The Carpet and Rug Institute (CRI) publishes a maintenance standard with the same logic: an extractor uses “pressure, water/cleaning solution, and suction” to remove deep soil; deep cleaning must extract with a certified system and allow drying with ventilation; “cleaning is not complete until carpet is dry and ready to be trafficked.” CRI 204 [21]

The IICRC recognises five broad methods:

  1. Hot-water extraction (HWE) — often called “steam cleaning”, but usually hot water, not steam. Heated water/detergent is injected under pressure and immediately vacuumed back with the soil. This is the restorative deep-clean baseline.
  2. Dry compound — an absorbent carrier is worked into the pile and vacuumed out. Very low moisture.
  3. Encapsulation — a detergent surrounds soil and crystallises as it dries; the crystals are vacuumed away. Low moisture, interim maintenance.
  4. Bonnet / absorbent pad — a pad absorbs soil with minimal moisture; often interim, and it can leave residue if not rinsed.
  5. Carpet shampooing — a foam/shampoo method followed by extraction.

National Carpet Cleaning Authority [22] · CMM [23]

For this rug, the relevant professional method is HWE at moderate temperature with strong moisture recovery, or a low-moisture encapsulation/compound method if the backing is fragile. Full immersion rug washing is a different trade — rug washing plants — and is the method most likely to conflict with IKEA’s “do not wash.” That last point is my inference: IKEA separates “do not dry clean” from “professional carpet cleaning” but does not define the latter. It is consistent with the carpet-cleaning standard and with the fact that the rug has a rubber backing. Rug-washing plants themselves often describe full immersion as the preferred method for rugs generally; see, for example, Brio Carpet Care [24]. That is not the right default for this construction. Ochoco, a specialist rug retailer, puts it well: many professional rug cleaners use low-moisture hot water extraction on polypropylene, but “repeated saturation can weaken the adhesive” on a latex backing, and you should ask for low-moisture HWE “rather than excessive heat.” Ochoco [9]

The professional sequence

A typical HWE job looks like this (synthesised from Voda’s 11-step description, the CRI/IICRC process, and Kärcher’s extraction guide):

  1. Pre-inspection — fibre, backing, construction, soil type, stains, damage, colourfastness, previous treatments.
  2. Protect the surroundings — move furniture, protect edges.
  3. Pre-vacuum — remove dry particulate soil. This is the most important step; wetting dry soil makes mud.
  4. Spot and stain treatment — appropriate chemistry for the specific spot.
  5. Pre-spray — diluted detergent; dwell 5–15 minutes.
  6. Agitation — soft brush, CRB, or pad machine.
  7. Extraction — inject water/solution and immediately vacuum it back, overlapping passes, then dry passes.
  8. Rinse — clean-water or acid/neutralising rinse to remove detergent residue.
  9. Dry — air movers, dehumidifier, ventilation; dry the pile and the backing.
  10. Post-inspection — check for wicking, streaks, damage, dryness; groom and vacuum.

Voda [25] · Kärcher [26] · CRI 204 [21]

What a professional adds that you cannot fully reproduce

  • Diagnosis — fibre/backing identification and method selection.
  • Chemistry control — correct detergent, dilution, pH, dwell, and rinse.
  • Pressure and recovery — truck-mount or high-lift portable extraction recovers far more water than a household vacuum.
  • Drying control — air movers/dehumidifiers and a defined dry-time target.
  • Liability and insurance — the cleaner carries the risk if something goes wrong.

For a 1.33 m² low-pile PP rug, the first two can be approximated with care; the third can be approximated with a rented extractor or a wet/dry shop vac; the fourth is a fan and patience; the fifth is the real reason to hire someone.

“Steam” is a confusing word

Consumer guides say “steam clean”; professional guides say “hot water extraction”. They are not the same. True steam is vapour above 100 °C and can distort PP. HWE uses heated water (often 50–70 °C in practice; truck mounts can be hotter) injected and immediately extracted. Sutherlandshire: “Use hot water extraction (not steam) for professional cleaning of polypropylene. Ask the cleaner to set equipment temperatures appropriately.” Sutherlandshire [15] Do not use a steam mop, wallpaper steamer, or kettle steam on this rug.

4. The private-person protocol

Decision tree

  • One or two spots, no overall dinginess → spot clean (Tier 1).
  • Overall grey/dull, oily traffic area, or faint odour → deep clean (Tier 2 or 3).
  • Backing cracked, peeling, sticky, or rug wrinkled → do not wet-clean; spot clean only, or replace.
  • Pet urine/odour that has reached the backing, or large/old stains → professional assessment; a surface clean may not reach the source.
  • You want a guarantee or cannot risk the rug → hire a professional (Tier 4).

Tier 1 — Spot cleaning (no special equipment)

Tools: white paper towels or clean white cloths, a spray bottle, a soft brush or sponge, lukewarm water, a mild clear detergent or a carpet spotter, a fan.

  1. Remove dry matter first. Scrape dried residue gently toward the centre with a plastic card or the back of a spoon; vacuum. IKEA: “Tørre pletter fjernes straks ved at skrabe forsigtigt mod midten af pletten.”
  2. Blot, do not rub. Absorb as much liquid as possible with paper towels, working from the outside in. IKEA: “Gnid ikke på våde pletter.”
  3. Test. Apply the solution to a hidden edge or the back; wait 5 minutes; blot with a white cloth. Check for colour transfer, backing softening, or pile change.
  4. Apply foam, not a flood. Mix a small amount of mild detergent with lukewarm water and whip it to foam, or use a carpet spotter per label. Apply the foam to a cloth and dab it onto the spot. Bolius recommends thick foam because it lifts soil and prevents it from sinking into the backing: “En tyk skum opsamler nemlig smudsen, og forhindrer i højere grad, at det synker ned i tæppet.” Bolius [27]
  5. Dwell 1–5 minutes. Do not let the solution dry on the rug.
  6. Agitate gently. Use a soft brush or sponge in the pile direction; do not scrub hard.
  7. Rinse. Blot with a clean cloth dampened with plain lukewarm water. Repeat until no soap residue remains. Residue attracts new dirt. Milliken: “Over application of detergent can lead to a detergent residue being left on the fibres, which will attract rapid resoiling.” Milliken [28]
  8. Dry. Blot with dry white towels; weight them for 10 minutes; air-dry with a fan. Do not walk on the spot until dry.
  9. For grease/oil: use a drop of clear dish soap directly on the spot, work gently, blot, rinse. PP is oleophilic; avoid oil-based solvents (WD-40, lighter fluid, eucalyptus oil). Sutherlandshire [15]
  10. For pet urine: blot, then an enzymatic cleaner per label; do not saturate; rinse and dry. If the odour returns, the backing/pad may be contaminated; consider a pro.

Tier 2 — Whole-rug deep clean with a wet/dry shop vacuum

This is the “no rental” version. It is suitable for a 1.33 m² rug if you already own a wet/dry vacuum. If you do not own one, a rented extractor (Tier 3) is usually better than buying a shop vac for one rug.

Tools: wet/dry shop vacuum with a wide nozzle or upholstery nozzle, clean garden sprayer or spray bottle, soft brush, white towels, shower squeegee, fan(s), dehumidifier (optional), pH-neutral/low-foam carpet cleaner or a small amount of clear dish soap.

Safety: use a vacuum rated for liquids, with the correct wet filter; never use an ordinary dry vacuum. Keep cords and outlets away from the wet area, wear gloves, and ventilate. Do not mix cleaning chemicals.

  1. Prepare the room. Remove furniture and the anti-slip underlay. Vacuum the floor. Work in a dry, well-ventilated room; avoid humid days.
  2. Pre-vacuum both sides. Use the standard floorhead, never the rotating brush (IKEA). Slow overlapping passes; vacuum the back too, because grit collects there.
  3. Pre-treat spots using Tier 1.
  4. Pre-spray lightly. Fill a clean sprayer with lukewarm water and carpet cleaner at the label’s minimum dose, or a few drops of clear dish soap per litre. Mist a 1 m² section from 20–30 cm away; the pile should be evenly damp, not saturated.
  5. Dwell 5–10 minutes. Do not let it dry. Milliken recommends about ten minutes for a pre-spray; more than that and it begins to dry out. Milliken [28]
  6. Agitate. Work a soft brush through the pile in overlapping strokes. Do not use a stiff deck brush or a rotary machine.
  7. Extract. With the wet/dry vac, use slow overlapping passes; then 2–3 dry-only passes. A shower squeegee can help push water toward the nozzle. SoCal Steam: “Wet/dry vac: press firmly and move slowly; repeat until minimal moisture is recovered”; “1 wet pass followed by 2–3 dry-only passes.” SoCal Steam [29]
  8. Rinse. Mist plain lukewarm water lightly over the section; extract again. Repeat until the recovered water is clear and no suds appear.
  9. Dry flat, elevated, with airflow on both sides. Blot with towels first. Lay the rug on a clean tarp or two sawhorses/drying racks; run a fan across it and, if available, a dehumidifier. Flip after 4–6 hours. Target 6–12 hours, and do not put it back until the backing is dry.
  10. Finish. When bone dry, vacuum both sides and brush the pile in one direction.

Tier 3 — Whole-rug deep clean with a rented extractor

This is the closest DIY equivalent to professional HWE.

  • Danish rental: Silvan “Tæpperenser, inkl. mundstykke”, 10 L clean / 9 L dirty tank, 239.95 DKK for 4 hours or 274.95 DKK for a day; cleaning fluid sold separately (75.95 DKK for 0.5 L, 139.95 DKK for 1 L). Bauhaus/XL Byg around 219 DKK for 4 hours; deposit often 520 DKK (refundable). Silvan [6] · Rentay [30]
  • Use the hand/upholstery tool, not a rotating floor brush. Keep the solution flow low.
  • Fill with lukewarm water and carpet cleaner at the minimum dose. Do not use the hot/steam setting if the machine has one. Ochoco: ask for “low-moisture hot water extraction … rather than excessive heat.” Ochoco [9]
  • Pre-vacuum, pre-spray, dwell, agitate, extract, rinse, dry as in Tier 2.
  • Make 1 wet pass and 2–3 dry passes per section; overlap; do not go over the same spot repeatedly “until it looks clean”. Over-wetting is the main failure mode.
  • Empty and rinse the recovery tank; clean the nozzle; dry the machine before returning it.

Tier 4 — Hire a professional

For this rug, this is often the rational choice because the rug is small (1.33 m²). Ask for in-home low-moisture extraction or a synthetic-rug specialist with controlled drying. Avoid a full-immersion rug wash unless the provider can explain how it protects the rubber backing.

6. When to hire, and what to ask

Hire a professional if:

  • the whole rug is dingy, grey, or oily, not just a few spots;
  • there is a persistent odour or pet contamination that may have reached the backing;
  • a stain remains after two careful spot-cleaning attempts;
  • the backing is cracked, peeling, sticky, or the rug is wrinkled/delaminated;
  • you want a written guarantee or are worried about damaging the rug.

Ask:

  1. What method will you use — low-moisture HWE, encapsulation, dry compound, or full immersion?
  2. What water temperature and pressure?
  3. What chemistry and pH, and how will you rinse it out?
  4. How will you protect the synthetic rubber backing from excess water and heat?
  5. How will you dry it, and what is the target dry time?
  6. Will you test colourfastness and the backing before starting?
  7. Are you insured for damage to the rug and the floor?

If the answer is “we wash it in a plant / immerse it”, that is a rug washing service. On the conservative reading, it is not the low-moisture carpet cleaning IKEA’s instruction points to for this rubber-backed construction. Ask how they handle rubber-backed rugs; if they cannot answer, choose another provider.

Machine wash / soak / full immersion IKEA: “Kan ikke vaskes”; rubber backing weakens when wet

Steam mop / high-heat steam Can melt/distort PP and stress the backing
Tumble dry / iron / hot hairdryer Heat damage to PP and rubber

8. Confidence, disagreements, and uncertainty

High confidence

  • “Do not wash” means no machine washing / full immersion; IKEA’s own FAQ says “Do not machine wash”, and the product page explicitly allows damp spot cleaning.
  • “Do not dry clean” is separate; “professional carpet cleaning” is water-based, not solvent dry cleaning.
  • The professional process is pre-vacuum → spot treat → pre-spray → dwell → agitate → extract → rinse → dry; this is consistent across IICRC/CRI, equipment manufacturers, and trade guides.
  • Heat, over-wetting, detergent residue, and oil-based solvents are the main avoidable failure modes.

Medium confidence

  • The exact backing polymer is undisclosed. The water/heat sensitivity evidence is about latex/SBR-backed carpet generally, not this specific rug. The safe assumption is conservative: no soaking, no heat, fast drying.
  • The exact safe water temperature for this backing is not published. “Lukewarm” and “low-moisture” are prudent; “hot” and “steam” are not.
  • Prices are a 5 November 2026 snapshot and vary by store, distance, minimum charge, and whether pickup/delivery is included.

Genuine disagreements in the sources

  • Generic PP guides say “hose it down” / “fully saturate”; IKEA says “do not wash.” The disagreement is resolved by distinguishing the face fibre from the backed construction.
  • Some sources say “steam cleaning is fine” for PP; others say “never steam.” The disagreement is mostly terminology: true steam is risky; moderate-temperature hot-water extraction is the professional method.
  • Some sources recommend dish soap; trade sources warn about residue. Dish soap is acceptable for a spot clean if used sparingly and rinsed; for a whole rug, use a low-residue carpet cleaner.
  • Rug-washing plants recommend full immersion for area rugs; that is appropriate for many wool rugs but conflicts with this rug’s “do not wash” label. Prefer in-home low-moisture extraction.

What would change the answer

  • A statement from IKEA naming the backing polymer and its moisture/heat limits.
  • A care sheet that explicitly says “professional hot-water extraction only” or “do not use a wet extractor.”
  • A laboratory test of this rug’s backing after wet cleaning.
  • A local professional who can show a documented method for rubber-backed PP rugs.

Until then, the conservative reading is the right one: spot-clean with minimal moisture, deep-clean only when necessary, use a low-moisture extraction method, and dry fast. For a 1.33 m² rug, compare the professional quote with the rental cost before deciding to do it yourself.

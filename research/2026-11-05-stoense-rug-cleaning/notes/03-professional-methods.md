# What “professional carpet cleaning” actually is

## It is a process standard, not a brand or a single machine

The relevant standard is **ANSI/IICRC S100, Standard for Professional Cleaning of Textile
Floor Coverings**. IICRC describes the scope as:

> “the procedures, methods, and systems to be followed when performing professional
> commercial and residential textile floor coverings (e.g., carpet and rugs) maintenance and
> cleaning.”

It lists the components: principles of cleaning; chemistry; fibres, construction and styles;
soil management; equipment and tools; methods and systems; safety and health; administrative
procedures; pre- and post-cleaning inspections; spot and stain removal; residential cleaning;
area rugs.

URL: https://iicrc.org/s100

The **Carpet and Rug Institute (CRI)** publishes a maintenance standard (CRI 204) with the
same logic. Key definitions:

> “Extractor - a type of cleaning equipment that uses pressure, water/cleaning solution, and
> suction to extract deeply embedded soil or stains from carpeting.”
> “Interim Maintenance System - a cleaning system comprised of an encapsulate solution, a
> CRB, and a vacuum.”
> “Extract using a SOA-certified deep cleaning system.”
> “Allow to dry. Supply natural or mechanical ventilation during the cleaning and drying
> phases of deep cleaning.”
> “Cleaning is not complete until carpet is dry and ready to be trafficked.”

URL: https://carpet-rug.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/CRI-204-Commercial.pdf

## The methods a professional may use

The IICRC S100 recognises five broad methods (summary from National Carpet Cleaning
Authority, https://nationalcarpetcleaningauthority.com/types-of-carpet-cleaning-methods.html):

1. **Hot-water extraction (HWE)** — often called “steam cleaning”, but usually hot water, not
   steam. Heated water/detergent is injected under pressure and immediately vacuumed back
   with the soil. This is the restorative deep-clean baseline.
2. **Dry compound** — an absorbent carrier is worked into the pile and vacuumed out. Very low
   moisture; good where water is risky.
3. **Encapsulation** — a detergent surrounds soil and crystallises as it dries; the crystals
   are vacuumed away. Low moisture, interim maintenance.
4. **Bonnet / absorbent pad** — a pad is used to absorb soil with minimal moisture; often
   interim maintenance, and it can leave residue if not rinsed.
5. **Carpet shampooing** — a foam/shampoo method, followed by extraction.

For this rug, the relevant professional method is **HWE at moderate temperature with strong
moisture recovery**, or a **low-moisture encapsulation/compound** method if the backing is
fragile. Full immersion rug washing is a different trade (rug washing plants) and is the
method most likely to conflict with IKEA’s “do not wash.”

## The process, step by step

A typical professional HWE job (synthesised from Voda’s 11-step description,
https://myvoda.com/carpet-cleaning-process, and the CRI/IICRC process) is:

1. **Pre-inspection** — identify fibre, backing, construction, soil type, stains, damage,
   colourfastness, and previous treatments. Choose the method and chemistry.
2. **Protect the surroundings** — move furniture, protect walls/floor edges.
3. **Pre-vacuum** — remove dry particulate soil. This is the most important step and is
   mandatory before wet cleaning; wetting dry soil makes mud.
4. **Spot and stain treatment** — treat specific spots with the appropriate chemistry.
5. **Pre-spray** — apply a diluted detergent to the pile; allow dwell time (often 5–15
   minutes) so it can suspend oils and soil.
6. **Agitation** — work the solution into the pile with a soft brush, CRB, or pad machine.
7. **Extraction** — inject hot water/solution and immediately vacuum it back, overlapping
   passes, followed by dry passes to recover moisture.
8. **Rinse** — a clean-water or acid/neutralising rinse to remove detergent residue.
9. **Dry** — air movers, dehumidifiers, ventilation; dry both the pile and the backing.
10. **Post-inspection** — check for wicking, streaks, damage, and dryness; groom the pile;
    vacuum when dry.

Kärcher’s professional guide describes the same spray-extraction logic:

> “The spray-extraction cleaner pressurises the cleaning solution into the carpet so that the
> dirt can be loosened and then sucked off again as a solution with dirt.”
> “rinse the surface once or twice afterwards with clean water.”
> “ensure good ventilation or even accelerate the drying process with an air blower.”

URL: https://www.karcher.com/int/professional/know-how/basic-carpet-cleaning.html

## What “professional” adds beyond a consumer machine

- **Diagnosis** — fibre/backing identification and method selection. A wrong method can ruin
  a rug that a consumer machine would clean safely.
- **Chemistry control** — correct detergent, dilution, pH, dwell, and rinse. Over-application
  leaves sticky residue that attracts new dirt.
- **Pressure and recovery** — truck-mount or high-lift portable extraction recovers far more
  water than a household vacuum.
- **Drying control** — air movers/dehumidifiers and a defined dry-time target.
- **Liability and insurance** — the cleaner carries the risk if something goes wrong.

For a 1.33 m² low-pile PP rug, the first two can be approximated with care; the third can be
approximated with a rented extractor or a wet/dry shop vac; the fourth is a fan and patience;
the fifth you cannot reproduce, which is the real reason to hire someone.

## The “steam” terminology trap

Consumer guides say “steam clean” and professional guides say “hot water extraction”. They
are not the same:

- **True steam** is vapour above 100 °C at atmospheric pressure; it is used for sanitising
  hard surfaces and can distort PP.
- **HWE** uses heated water (often 50–70 °C in practice; truck mounts can be hotter) injected
  under pressure and immediately extracted. It is not a dry vapour.

Sutherlandshire:
> “Use hot water extraction (not steam) for professional cleaning of polypropylene. Ask the
> cleaner to set equipment temperatures appropriately.”

Ochoco:
> “many professional rug cleaners routinely use low-moisture hot water extraction on
> polypropylene rugs because it removes embedded dirt more effectively than surface cleaning
> alone.”
> “For professional cleaning, choose a company experienced with synthetic rugs, and ask them
> to use a low-moisture hot water extraction process rather than excessive heat.”

URL: https://www.ochoco.com.au/blogs/polypropylene-rug-material-guides/how-to-clean-polypropylene-rugs

## What a private person should ask a professional

1. What method will you use — low-moisture HWE, encapsulation, dry compound, or full
   immersion?
2. What water temperature and pressure?
3. What chemistry and pH, and how will you rinse it out?
4. How will you protect the synthetic rubber backing from excess water and heat?
5. How will you dry it, and what is the target dry time?
6. Will you test colourfastness and the backing before starting?
7. Are you insured for damage to the rug and the floor?

If the answer is “we wash it in a plant / immerse it”, that is a **rug washing** service, not
the low-moisture carpet cleaning IKEA’s instruction points to. Ask how they handle rubber-
backed rugs; if they cannot answer, choose another provider.

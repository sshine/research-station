# Material constraints: polypropylene face, synthetic rubber backing

## Polypropylene (olefin) face fibre

### What it is good at

- Hydrophobic: it takes up very little water, so water-based spills sit on the surface and
  blot up easily. Sources: Ochoco, “How to Clean Polypropylene Rugs”
  (https://www.ochoco.com.au/blogs/polypropylene-rug-material-guides/how-to-clean-polypropylene-rugs);
  Masterful Carpet Cleaning, “Olefin Carpet Cleaning”
  (https://masterfulcarpetcleaning.com/olefin-carpet-cleaning).
- Solution-dyed: colour is in the polymer, so it is unusually resistant to fading and to
  dilute bleach at the fibre level. Source: Sutherlandshire Cleaners
  (https://sutherlandshirecleaners.com/blog/polypropylene-carpet-cleaning-guide).
- Resistant to most acids and alkalis. Source: Cleaning Systems Ltd
  (https://cleaningsystems.co.nz/knowledge/test-knowledge-1-2-2-2-3).

### What it is bad at

- **Oleophilic.** It attracts and holds oils. Cleaning Systems:
  > “Polypropylene is oleophilic. That means that it has a strong natural affinity for oils.
  > It attracts oily soils. Oily soils and spills can be difficult to remove and when left
  > soiled for an extended time, this may cause discolouration.”
  Bane-Clene makes the same point:
  > “Olefin fibers are frequently difficult to clean because of their tenacious affinity for
  > oils.”
  URL: https://www.baneclene.com/pages/olefinpreconditioner
- **Heat-sensitive.** INEOS’s technical data sheet gives a DSC melting point of
  **160–165 °C** for homopolymer polypropylene and a Vicat softening temperature of
  **152 °C**:
  https://www.ineos.com/globalassets/ineos-group/businesses/ineos-olefins-and-polymers-usa/products/technical-information--patents/ineos-engineering-properties-of-pp.pdf
  Fibre-grade PP is similar: Swicofil gives a softening point “in the region of 150 °C” and a
  melting point of “160–170 °C”, and notes that below 120 °C the fibre “nearly remain[s]”
  normal mechanical properties:
  https://old.swicofil.com/pp.html
  These are far above normal cleaning temperatures, but they explain why an iron, a hot
  hairdryer held close, a steam mop, or friction heat from a rotary brush can permanently
  distort the pile. Sutherlandshire:
  > “high-temperature steam from a steam mop or carpet steamer can partially melt and
  > distort polypropylene pile, causing permanent matting or texture change.”
- **Poor crush recovery.** PP has low resilience; heavy furniture and traffic can flatten it,
  and some flattening is permanent. Cleaning Systems:
  > “they have some resiliency problems and tend to mat and crush in high-traffic areas, no
  > matter how good you are at cleaning carpet, crushing or matting is not restorable.”
- **Oil-based solvents can make things worse.** Sutherlandshire warns that WD-40, lighter
  fluid, eucalyptus oil and similar solvents may lift a stain but leave a new oil residue.
  Cleaning Systems warns that some organic solvents such as citrus solvent may adversely
  affect PP.

### Consequence for cleaning

- Use **water-based, mild, low-residue chemistry**, not solvent-heavy spot cleaners.
- For grease/oil, use a **surfactant** (a small amount of clear dish soap or a carpet
  degreaser) and rinse thoroughly; do not use an oil-based solvent.
- Keep **heat low**. Warm water is enough; steam is not appropriate.
- Do not scrub hard or use a stiff brush; PP is abrasion-resistant but the pile can fuzz,
  distort, or mat.

## Synthetic rubber backing

IKEA does not disclose the polymer. The care label says only “100 % Syntetisk gummi”. The
most common carpet-backing synthetic rubbers are styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) latex,
carboxylated SBR, or thermoplastic rubber blends. Treat the following as **class-level
evidence**, not a specification for this rug.

- C&R magazine, “Understanding the Construction of Carpet”:
  > “a carpet’s latex adhesive loses its strength when wet, but its strength returns once
  > dried.”
  > “latex … fillers … do not respond well to excessive water and tend to soften when
  > saturated.”
  > Causes of delamination include “overly aggressive extraction with improper tools”,
  > “excessive folding or stretching of carpet during the restoration process”, and
  > “excessive heat from carpet cleaning”.
  URL: https://www.candrmagazine.com/understanding-the-construction-of-carpet-why-it-matters
- NC State Extension, “Water-Damaged Carpets and Rugs”:
  > “Moisture can weaken the latex backing and walking on the carpet can cause the backing to
  > separate. When the backing dries, it regains most of its original strength.”
  URL: https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/water-damaged-carpets-and-rugs
- Ochoco, on polypropylene rugs:
  > “Be more cautious if: The rug has a latex backing, since repeated saturation can weaken
  > the adhesive over time.”
  > “The fibre itself won’t absorb much water, but the backing and the subfloor underneath
  > can still be affected.”
  URL: https://www.ochoco.com.au/blogs/polypropylene-rug-material-guides/how-to-clean-polypropylene-rugs
- RugPadUSA, on rubber-backed rugs:
  > “It’s not breathable. Thus, a latex-backed carpet traps a lot of moisture between the rug
  > and the floor which subsequently causes dampness and discoloration.”
  URL: https://www.rugpadusa.com/articles/are-rubber-backed-rugs-right-for-your-floors
- Spincycle, on washing rubber-backed rugs:
  > “Use cold water, or warm at most, but never hot. Cold water is safest to prevent damage
  > or shrinkage.”
  > “Be careful not to wring or twist the rug, as that can crack the rubber backing.”
  > “Lay it flat to dry … flip the rug every 12 hours.”
  URL: https://spincyclelaundrychicago.com/can-you-wash-rubber-backed-rugs-in-the-washing-machine

### Consequence for cleaning

- The **face fibre tolerates water**; the **backing governs** how much water and heat are
  safe.
- Avoid full immersion, soaking, wringing, hanging wet by one edge, and slow drying.
- Extract as much water as possible, dry flat with airflow on both sides, and do not put the
  rug back on a wood/vinyl floor until the backing is dry.
- Heat is doubly bad: it can soften PP and accelerate backing/adhesive degradation.
- If the backing is already cracked, peeling, sticky, or delaminating, do not wet-clean;
  spot clean only and consider replacement.

## The “PP can be hosed down” conflict

Many generic guides say polypropylene rugs can be hosed down, fully saturated, or even
machine washed. Example: Rug Branch
(https://www.rugbranch.com/blogs/rugs/how-to-clean-polypropylene-rug) says “Polypropylene
handles getting fully saturated without any fibre damage” and gives a full hose-down method.
That statement is about the **face fibre**. It ignores the backing. For a rug whose care label
says “Do not wash” and whose backing is synthetic rubber, the manufacturer’s construction-
specific instruction outranks the generic fibre advice. This is the central disagreement in
the source set; see `06-disagreements-and-uncertainty.md`.

## What this means for “professional” cleaning

A professional cleaner who says “polypropylene is waterproof, we can wash it” may be right
about the yarn and wrong about the rug. The correct question is not “what can PP tolerate?”
but “what can this **backed construction** tolerate?” That is why low-moisture extraction and
controlled drying are the relevant professional methods, not full immersion.
